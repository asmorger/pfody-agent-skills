One Hour Per Slice

You are being asked to specify a system that nobody fully remembers how to describe. You don't have to. Most of what we need is already recorded in the old application, and we can read it out ourselves.

What we can't read out is what should be different. That part is yours, and it is much smaller than it looks. This page is what we need from you, when we need it, and what you get back each time.

There are two kinds of unknown, and they get confused

“We don't know the requirements” is doing double duty right now. It covers two very different problems, and only one of them is waiting on you.

We can answer this

~80%

What does the app do today?

It's in the code, the database, the stored procedures, and twelve years of production logs. Nobody has to remember it and nobody has to decide it. It is a research task, and it is the thing our tooling is genuinely good at. When you can't tell us what the invoice screen does, that is not a gap we are blocked on.

Only you can answer this

~20%

What should be different?

Which of today's behaviors are correct, which are bugs we've been living with, and which nobody needs any more. This is real product judgment and we cannot substitute for it. It is also a much shorter list than a full specification, and we will always bring it to you as a list, never as a blank page.

The practical consequence: we stop asking you to describe the system and start asking you to rule on findings. Those are different cognitive tasks, and the second one is far easier and far more accurate. Nobody can fill a blank page. Everybody can tell you that item four is wrong.

What arrives in your inbox

Read the evidence line before you answer. “Found in code” means we verified it. “Found in a conversation” means someone told us. “Found in a guess” means we are genuinely unsure and your answer carries more weight than ours. Mixing those three together without labels is exactly how large requirements documents mislead everybody, so we keep them separate and visible.

The log counts are there to help you delete things. Nothing shortens a project faster than finding out a screen was opened four times last year.

Defer is a real answer. It does not lose the item and it is not a failure. It moves the item back onto the findings list so the slice can start this week instead of waiting for an answer that needs a person who is on leave. Five deferred items and one shipped slice beats zero of both.

Why we are not writing the full specification first

This is the part most worth your scrutiny, because the alternative feels safer and isn't. The question is not how fast we can write things down. It is how many times, over the life of the project, a wrong assumption gets caught.

There is a second reason, specific to how we are building this. Our tooling multiplies output on work that is clearly defined and multiplies rework on work that is vague. Pointing it at a 200-page document written months before anyone tested an assumption produces a great deal of confidently wrong software, quickly. The clarity has to arrive just before the building, not long before it.

How we make switching over safe

“Ship every two weeks” should make you nervous if it means users get exposed to half-finished work every two weeks. It doesn't. Three things sit between a finished slice and a user noticing anything.

Automated checks

Before we write new code, we drive the old application and record what it does as automated tests. The new code has to produce the same answers. If it doesn't, we find out in minutes, not in a UAT cycle.

A side-by-side run

For a few days the new path runs in production alongside the old one, on real inputs, and we compare every result. Users are still on the old path the whole time. This catches the inputs nobody thought to imagine.

A switch, not a launch

Going live is one setting, flipped per workflow, with the old path still running underneath. If something is wrong, it flips back the same day. No release, no weekend, no cutover event.

This is also why you are only asked for ten minutes at the switch. By the time it reaches you, the question is not “do you believe this works” but “here are the comparison numbers from four days of real traffic, and the differences are zero — shall we turn it on?”

What this genuinely asks of you

Being straight about the demands, because three of them cut against normal product instinct.

  • Decisions on a clock, with incomplete information. You will be asked to rule on things you would rather research. What protects you is not better research, it is that each ruling covers one slice and can be revisited in two weeks. Deciding wrongly and finding out quickly is cheaper here than deciding well and slowly.
  • Saying “drop it” out loud. Legacy apps accumulate behavior nobody chose. Dropping it needs someone with authority to say so, and the usage numbers will usually back you up. Every drop is a feature we don't build, test, or support again.
  • Letting us tell you we were wrong. Sometimes we will report that the old app does not do what our findings list said. That is the process working as designed and it should not read as a quality problem. The alternative is that we are wrong and nobody notices for five months.
  • No single document describing the finished system. What accumulates instead is a growing set of automated checks, which is more reliable than prose because it cannot quietly go out of date. If you need a narrative document for a steering committee or an auditor, say so and we will write one deliberately, for that audience, on a schedule. We just won't pretend it is the source of truth.

What you own, that we can't do for you

  1. The order. Which workflow we modernize next. We will tell you which ones are cheapest and best understood; the call is yours.
  2. The rulings. Keep, change, drop, or defer, with your name on each one.
  3. The switch. Approving each flip once the comparison numbers are in front of you.
  4. Access to the one person who knows. Occasionally a finding needs a specific long-tenured user or the controller. Getting us twenty minutes of their time is worth more than a week of our guessing.

What you can hold us to

Two numbers, both checkable without taking our word for anything.

  • Something works every two weeks. Not a demo of a prototype. A real workflow, running in production, switchable. If a slice slips past two weeks, that is a signal we sized it wrong, and we owe you the reason.
  • The findings list shrinks as the test suite grows. Each slice retires a chunk of the old app and leaves behind permanent automated checks. If after three months the open findings are not going down and the checks are not going up, something is wrong with how we are working and you are entitled to say so.

And one thing to expect in the first few weeks: the earliest work produces no visible features at all. We are reading the old system and building the machinery that compares old against new. It is the investment that makes every later slice safe to get wrong, and it is the main reason the second month moves faster than the first.
