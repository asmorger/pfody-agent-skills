Survey, Slice, Suite

You asked for two documents. There are two you write, and one you accumulate. The one you accumulate is the only one that lasts, and it is the only one that can fail.

A PRD does the damage it does because it is permanent, wide, one-directional, and unfalsifiable. Take away any one of those and it stops being a PRD. Here all four go: the survey is wide but disposable and derived, the brief is authored but narrow and short-lived, and the suite is permanent but executable.

The two you write

The field names matter less than the two properties that separate them: who is allowed to change them, and when they die. Those are the invariants. Everything else is yours to adjust.

  • Every line carries provenance. [code:], [db:], [told:], [assumed:]. This is the whole trick. An unsourced assumption on page 30 of a PRD reads exactly like a verified fact, and an agent will implement both with equal confidence.
  • Never hand-edited to record a decision. The moment a ruling lands here, it is a PRD again and nobody can tell observation from wish. Decisions go to the brief.
  • Wrong is cheap. If it is wrong, regenerate it. It is allowed to be wide precisely because it costs nothing to rebuild and nothing to throw away.
  • Questions are multiple choice. Your PM cannot fill a blank page. Almost nobody can. They can tell you that Q1 is a bug and Q3 is obsolete, in about four minutes.

  • One demo sentence, in the user's words. If you cannot write it without a conjunction, the slice is two slices.
  • Decisions outstanding must read 0 to start. This is the gate that replaces the requirements meeting. Anything unresolved becomes a DEFER row and leaves the slice.
  • Blast radius, not size, is what makes it cheap. A two-hour change carrying a schema migration is not cheap to be wrong about. A two-week change behind a toggle with the legacy path still hot is.
  • Frozen at build start, append-only after. New learning goes in an ## Amendments section with a date and a decider. Editing the brief in place erases the record you are trying to build.

The learning cycle

“Cheap enough to be wrong” is only meaningful if wrongness has somewhere to go. Four things can tell you a premise was wrong, and they differ by how long they take to speak up. The cost of correcting rises with that delay, which is the whole reason to invest in the fast ones.

Where each kind of wrongness lands. A loop without addresses is just a meeting.
What you learnWhere it landsWhat actually changes
The survey misread the legacy behaviorRegenerate the entry, fix the provenance tagThe survey. The brief is untouched, because no decision was wrong.
The legacy behavior is itself a bugAn amendment on the live brief, with a decider and a dateThis slice's scope, once. If it needs a second amendment, stop and reshape.
A behavior exists that nobody surveyedA new survey entry, then a new sliceThe backlog. The current slice still ships.
Appetite was wrong by more than 2×A findingHow you size the next slice. Never a renegotiation of this one.
The slice was too big to be wrong cheaplyA finding, then the slicing ruleThe shaping gate. Usually by adding a new entry to the blast-radius list.
The same ruling recurs across three slicesAn ADRA durable why. This is the only path by which a document earns permanence.
Something you dropped turns out to be neededA new sliceThe backlog. Never a retro-edit of a shipped brief.

Two health signals

Everything above is unfalsifiable on its own, which would make it exactly the thing it is trying to replace. These two numbers are the check.

The survey shrinks ↓

Entries are deleted at each flip and added only one area ahead. If survey/ is growing faster than it empties, you are surveying the whole app instead of the next slice.

The suite grows ↑

Every slice adds characterization tests that outlive it. This is the asset. It is the only artifact that encodes behavior in a form that can tell you it is wrong.

If neither moved

Six slices shipped, survey/ no smaller, suite no larger: the loop is not running. You have a document practice, not a delivery practice.

Five ways this rots into a PRD

  • Surveying everything before shaping anything. The survey is a just-in-time artifact too. Survey one area ahead. A complete survey of the whole app is a PRD that took agents to write instead of people.
  • Hand-editing the survey to record a ruling. Now observation and intention are indistinguishable, and the provenance tags are lying.
  • Writing characterization tests from the survey instead of from the running system. This one is quiet and fatal. Tests built on your misreading will pass, and you will have automated the misreading. Always derive them by exercising the legacy binary or its domain layer.
  • Editing a frozen brief in place. The amendment log is the learning. Smoothing it over leaves you with no evidence about how often your premises are wrong, which is the one number that tells you whether slices are sized right.
  • A slice with a migration in it. Irreversible, therefore not cheap to be wrong about, whatever the hour count says. Schema moves get their own slices, with their own backout.

Your first two weeks

  1. Pick the workflow with the highest log volume and the fewest unknowns, not the one the PM is loudest about. Survey it with agents. Two days.
  2. Stand up the characterization harness against the legacy binary or its domain layer, before any new code exists. This is the capital investment and it is the thing that actually buys you velocity later, because it is what makes every subsequent slice safe to get wrong.
  3. Take the survey's Questions section to the PM and BA as a markup exercise. Not an interview. Thirty minutes, and bring the usage numbers, because nothing deletes scope faster than “this screen was opened four times last year.”
  4. Shape exactly one slice with decisions outstanding at 0. Build it. Flip it behind a toggle with the legacy path still hot and the diff running.
  5. Delete the survey entry. Write one finding. Then do it again, and compare your appetite against what it actually cost.

One thing to say upward, once: agents multiply throughput on well-specified work and multiply waste on vague work. The constraint here is business decisions per week, which is currently near zero. Steps 1 and 2 need none of them, which is why they come first and why they are where the real compression is available.

Derived from XP's actual commitments rather than its artifacts: the oracle stands in for the on-site customer, characterization tests stand in for the story conversation, and the toggle plus the diff stand in for the weekly demo. The documents are allowed to exist because they are scheduled to die.
